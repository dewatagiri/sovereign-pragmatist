The Sovereign Pragmatist

Malaysia holds strong cards for the next decade. Whether it wins depends less on Penang’s cleanrooms than on a million small businesses learning to automate.

By Azman TaufikOctober 202612 min read

Idea in Brief

The Situation

The old global rulebook is weakening. Supply chains are being rebuilt for safety, not just cost. Neutral, capable countries like Malaysia gain bargaining power.

The Gap

Malaysia is winning outside but feeling strain inside: flat starting pay, a squeezed middle class, and MSMEs still running on manual work and cheap labour.

The Way Forward

Turn the coming labour and wage reforms into an automation drive for small firms — practical, affordable tools first — so higher pay comes from higher productivity.

For eighty years, emerging economies could plan around one assumption: a broadly predictable, rules-based global order, anchored by Western leadership, would keep trade open and disputes contained. That assumption no longer holds with the same force. Sanctions reach further than before. Reserve assets and payment systems have become tools of pressure. Tariffs and export controls are now everyday policy. Multilateral bodies struggle to settle the big disputes.

None of this means the world has become lawless. It means rules now matter less than resilience. Countries are judged by what they can produce, supply, and protect at home.

For middle powers, the lesson is clear. Tie yourself too tightly to one bloc and you risk being caught in its fights. Neglect your domestic base and you risk losing public consent for openness itself.

Malaysia is a good test case. Abroad, it has positioned itself as a trusted, non-aligned partner to all sides. At home, it must resolve a harder question: how an open, investment-rich economy delivers better pay and better prospects to the Malaysians who work in it — most of whom work in small businesses.

The Global Setting

Three powers, three pressures

The United States faces heavy debt-servicing costs and deep political division at home. Its posture has shifted from alliance leadership toward more transactional economic policy: tariffs, export controls on advanced technology, and the reach of the dollar payment system.

China, facing technology restrictions abroad and balance-sheet repair at home, is deepening its trade and industrial ties across the Global South. Its scale in clean technology, electric vehicles, and mature-node chips means few developing Asian economies can — or want to — decouple from it.

Europe and the United Kingdom are adjusting to higher energy costs, large industrial subsidies elsewhere, and rising defence needs. Their industrial base is under pressure, and they are looking for reliable new production partners.

Our neighbours are each choosing a different posture. Indonesia pairs an independent, “free and active” foreign policy with a push to process its own minerals at home. Singapore remains the region’s financial hub, careful to stay open to all sides. The Philippines has chosen closer security ties with the United States. Malaysia’s path sits between these: open to every partner, aligned with none.

The common thread: global manufacturing is moving from pure “just-in-time” efficiency to “just-in-case” redundancy. Companies want plants in more than one jurisdiction. That shift gives Southeast Asia — and Malaysia in particular — real bargaining power.

Part I · The External Strengths

Malaysia as the neutral clearinghouse

Malaysia enters the decade with three advantages it should keep building.

1The semiconductor back-end

Full US–China tech decoupling is not practical. Front-end chip fabrication is being re-shored in the West at high cost, but back-end assembly, packaging and testing remains global. Malaysia handles about 13% of the world’s back-end semiconductor assembly, testing and packaging, according to MIDA, and around 7% of global semiconductor trade.

The National Semiconductor Strategy, launched in 2024, sets clear targets: RM500 billion in investment by 2030, 60,000 high-skilled Malaysian engineers, and home-grown firms that can compete globally. Early signs are good. Semiconductor exports for January–April 2026 reached about RM212 billion, up from RM141 billion a year earlier. Malaysia is valuable to both Western and Chinese firms precisely because it is open to both.

2Power, land and the data-centre corridor

AI infrastructure needs reliable power, water, and land — which many established hubs cannot add quickly. Johor and the Klang Valley have captured a large share of this wave. Malaysia’s operational data-centre capacity is projected to reach about 2,055 MW by end-2026, roughly four times the level of early 2025.

Johor’s stricter approval rules — about 30% of applications reportedly turned down on sustainability grounds, and growing use of recycled water — show the right instinct. The goal is not more server halls for their own sake. It is a modern grid, local skills, and local suppliers that grow alongside them.

3Settling trade in our own currencies

Bank Negara Malaysia has steadily widened its Local Currency Settlement frameworks with China, Indonesia and Thailand, and in 2026 expanded local-currency arrangements with Japan alongside a US$6 billion swap line. Trade and investment payments settled under these frameworks reached about RM82 billion by November 2025, up from RM3.6 billion in 2009. With China, about a quarter of bilateral trade is now settled in ringgit and renminbi.

This is not about abandoning the dollar. It is about lower conversion costs and less exposure to someone else’s liquidity shock — a practical benefit for exporters of every size.

Part II · The Home Front

Where the strain is showing

Malaysia’s agility abroad contrasts with tougher conditions at home. The issues are well known. They are not anyone’s fault alone; they are the result of choices that made sense for their time.

A low-cost model that has run its course

For three decades, access to foreign workers helped Malaysian industry stay competitive in construction, plantations, manufacturing and services. Migrant workers built much of what we use every day, and many sectors still depend on them. But the model had a side effect: manual labour often stayed cheaper than machines and software, so many firms had little reason to invest in productivity.

The result shows up in starting pay. Many graduates and skilled school-leavers enter jobs whose pay has barely kept pace with living costs. Some move into gig work; some move abroad. Either way, the skills the semiconductor and digital economy need are harder to keep.

The government has already set the direction. The 13th Malaysia Plan aims to bring foreign workers down to 10% of the workforce by 2030 and 5% by 2035, supported by a multi-tier levy planned from 2026. Budget 2027 raises the minimum wage from RM1,700 to RM2,000 from June 2027, with MSMEs below RM50 million in annual sales exempt for now.

The question is no longer whether small firms will move away from cheap labour. It is whether they will get the tools to do it before the bill arrives.

The squeezed middle

Fiscal reform is replacing broad subsidies on fuel, electricity and staples with targeted support. That is sound policy. But households just above the support threshold feel it most: they earn too much for direct aid and too little to absorb higher costs comfortably. Spending power and confidence in this group matter, because they are the customers of most MSMEs.

A shared frustration — and a shared answer

Across communities, many working Malaysians share a sense that growth reaches mobile capital at the top faster than it reaches local workers and local businesses in the middle. This is a concern about fairness, not about foreigners. It is best answered not by closing doors, but by making sure the benefits of openness reach Malaysian workers, suppliers and small businesses more visibly.

Why MSMEs are the hinge

This is where the external and domestic stories meet. MSMEs produced 39.7% of GDP and employed 48.7% of the workforce — about 8.1 million people — in 2025, yet accounted for less than 15% of exports. Large investors bring the capital; small firms carry the jobs. If MSMEs cannot raise productivity, they cannot raise wages, and the gains from Penang and Johor will feel distant to most Malaysians.

Part III · The Roadmap

Practical pragmatism: four moves

Malaysia does not need a new ideology. It needs the reforms already announced to land well — so that wage and labour changes push firms toward productivity, not out of business.

1Make the multi-tier levy a bridge, not a penalty

A flat levy is treated as just another cost. A tiered levy — rising as a firm’s reliance on foreign workers goes above set thresholds — can change behaviour, but only if firms can see it coming and have a way out. Three design points matter:

Notice. Publish thresholds and rates well ahead, as industry has asked, with a phased start. Ring-fencing. Place the extra revenue from higher tiers into a dedicated, independently audited transition fund that co-finances automation for MSMEs and supports local hiring. Fairness by sector. Keep realistic allowances for “dirty, difficult and dangerous” work where machines are not yet a substitute.

2Define automation for the million, not the thousand

When policymakers say “automation”, they often picture robot arms on a factory floor. For most of Malaysia’s more than one million MSMEs, automation looks different. It is a WhatsApp assistant that answers enquiries and takes bookings at midnight. A website customers can find on Google. Digital bookkeeping and e-invoicing that cut the weekend paperwork. Scheduling that keeps a small team busy instead of waiting.

These tools are cheap, fast to set up, and free up hours every week — often the difference between hiring one more Malaysian or not. Budget 2026 set aside RM1 billion through development finance institutions for automation and digitalisation, and Budget 2027 funds MDEC to help 4,000 MSMEs adopt AI. That is a good start. The next step is reach: simple claim processes, support for subscription-based software (not just equipment purchases), and treating automation-as-a-service and equipment leasing as fully eligible costs, so firms without upfront capital can still take part.

For most small firms, automation is not a robot. It is the enquiry answered at midnight and the invoice sent without a weekend of paperwork.

3Use the wage window to upgrade, not just to wait

The minimum-wage exemption for smaller MSMEs buys time. It should be used. Linking the exemption period to simple productivity support — a digital health check, a short list of approved tools, and access to financing and guarantees, now expanded to RM57 billion for 2027 — would help firms arrive at the higher wage level ready, not shocked.

4Keep homes within reach while staying open

Foreign investment in property brings capital and jobs. It works best when it does not compete with first-time Malaysian buyers. States already set minimum price thresholds for foreign purchases; these can be sharpened by channelling foreign buyers toward higher-value, high-density developments, and by directing part of the related fees into transit-linked housing for local workers. The aim is simple: openness that local families can see working for them.

What this means for MSME owners — five steps for the next 12 months
  1. Answer every enquiry fast. Set up automatic WhatsApp replies and booking so no customer waits until morning.
  2. Get found. A basic website and an up-to-date Google Business Profile are now the shopfront.
  3. Get e-invoice ready. Move invoices and records into a simple digital system before the deadline forces it.
  4. Count the hours. List the tasks that eat your week. Automate the most repetitive one first.
  5. Plan for the wage step. Map your costs at RM2,000 minimum wage now, and use the exemption window to invest, not just to wait.

The Bottom Line

The next decade will not reward rigid positions. No external power, East or West, will put Malaysia’s stability ahead of its own interests — nor should we expect it to.

Malaysia’s path is a practical one: stay open and non-aligned abroad, and build a productive, fair home front. That means turning semiconductor and energy strengths into lasting bargaining power, and turning labour and wage reforms into an automation drive that small firms can afford. If the million small businesses that employ half the country can do more with the same hands, higher wages will follow — and Malaysia will come through this era not just intact, but stronger.
